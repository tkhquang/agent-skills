# Puzzles

Contents: what a puzzle is, required traits, where the fun comes from, types, level design as puzzle design, difficulty levers, review checklist.

## What a puzzle is

A puzzle is a non-combat task, or series of tasks, the player must complete to progress. It has a state, mechanics that change the state, a goal state and usually a theme. To a player, a puzzle they cannot solve is indistinguishable from a stop sign.

Include puzzles only when they support what the game is about. A riddle in the middle of a falling-block game or a logic grid in a fast shooter works against the core.

## Required traits

For any puzzle or mini-game:

- Affordances: the rules and controls are easy to work out.
- Identifiable patterns: colors, shapes or existing knowledge let the player see possibilities.
- Ease of use: the interface does not fight the solver.
- Rewards skill: a player who meets this kind of puzzle again does better.

For a puzzle inside a larger game, also:

- Immersion: it matches the game's style, story and world.
- Meaningful consequences outside the puzzle: more than "the door opens".

## Where the fun comes from

The pleasure is the "aha": elements that seemed unrelated suddenly form one meaning, and the world becomes more manageable. A meaningless string of letters becomes trivial to remember once you see it is the second and third letters of each month.

The designer's job is to guide the solver toward the connection.

- Build on experience. If the solution needs a novel use of an item, let the player use that item several times in ordinary ways first, and let them experiment freely.
- Keep classes of objects consistent. All crates of this kind break. All panels of this kind fail when wet. Consistency builds the vocabulary puzzles are written in.
- Unfold the solution as a chain of small ahas. After each correct step give an unmistakable sign that it was right: a tone, a movement, a reaction. It must clearly be a response to the player and not background noise.
- Let the player own the solution, even when you led them to it.

## Types

| Type | What the player does | Cautions |
|---|---|---|
| Riddle | Reinterprets wording to find the one answer | Expensive, used once, and you either know it or you are stuck. Make it optional, give clues, allow another route. Write the answer first, then the riddle |
| Lateral thinking | Drops a hidden assumption | Same cautions as riddles |
| Spatial reasoning | Moves and fits objects, in the mind or on the board | Scales well into whole games and boss encounters where the arena or the creature is the puzzle |
| Pattern recognition | Spots the rule in what is shown | Works for boss weak points and codes. Vary speed and small deviations |
| Logic | Derives new facts from given facts | Trial and error can bypass it, so add a cost for wrong guesses or a bonus for a first-try solve. Otherwise it is an arbitrary gate |
| Exploration | Finds the path, the person, the hidden thing | Enjoyable for its own sake. Becomes a stop sign when a blocked route has no alternative |
| Item use | Works out which object to use, on what, in what order | Combinations and non-obvious uses raise difficulty sharply. Clue by showing a character use the item earlier |

Short embedded mini-games (lock picking, hacking) follow the same rules.

## Level design is puzzle design

Both arrange existing mechanics to produce different dynamics. Fighting the same enemy in a long corridor and in a small room uses identical mechanics and plays completely differently. Crate puzzles, crosswords and number grids are all "placement of known pieces in a new arrangement".

## Difficulty levers

You cannot judge your own puzzle. You already know the answer, so it is always harder than it looks to you. Only watching fresh solvers shows the real difficulty.

A stuck player can try again, buy a guide, ask a friend, search online, or quit. None of these is fun. At the other extreme, a solution seen instantly that takes five minutes to carry out is busywork.

Levers, roughly from gentle to strong:

1. Add or remove clues. Successive hints lower difficulty without removing the solve.
2. Move clues. A clue beside the puzzle is easy to connect. A clue across the map is hard.
3. Add or improve feedback on wrong attempts. "It is very dark in there and you are afraid of the dark" beats "you can't go that way".
4. Allow multiple solutions, especially the wrong one many testers try. Or clue clearly why it fails.
5. Offer help that responds to play: after a failure, a nudge that keeps the first attempt unspoiled.
6. Adjust difficulty dynamically based on how the player is doing.
7. Remove linearity. A blocked player should always have another goal to pursue.
8. Add or remove a time limit.
9. Test on varied, new players, repeatedly.

Never fix a hard puzzle by putting its answer somewhere the player has to pay for.

## Review checklist

- Does the puzzle serve the core of the game?
- Can a new player tell what the controls and rules are within seconds?
- Has the player already met every element the solution needs?
- Is there feedback for each correct intermediate step?
- Is there informative feedback for the most common wrong attempts?
- Is there more than one way through, or something else to do while stuck?
- Does solving it change something the player cares about?
- Has someone who has never seen it solved it while you watched in silence?
