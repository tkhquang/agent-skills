# Ethics of persuasion in games

Contents: a framework for judging a mechanic, firm lines, random paid rewards, review checklist, how to respond to risky requests.

## A framework for judging a mechanic

Persuasion is not automatically wrong. Being likeable is persuasive. A sale is persuasive. Judge each design through the lenses below.

| Lens | Question | Guidance |
|---|---|---|
| Intent | What is this for? | Offering enjoyment in exchange for time and money is legitimate. Intending to extract from people who would regret it is not |
| Method | How does it work on the player? | Deception is always wrong. Conditioning through unpredictable rewards is a red flag, acceptable only when it is overt and harmless |
| Outcome | What happens to players, including the ones at the edges? | The intended outcome is time and money spent gladly. The predictable unintended outcome is some people spending time or money they cannot afford. Makers, platforms and players share responsibility, and makers go first |

Adults are entitled to spend their money and time as they choose with informed consent. Children and vulnerable people need protection that does not depend on their own judgment.

## Firm lines

The lines in SKILL.md apply. In detail:

- Deception includes hidden costs and an advertisement disguised as play.
- No dark patterns in purchase flows: confusing currencies that hide real prices, countdown pressure on first-time buyers, accidental-tap traps, hard-to-find refunds.
- Absence is never punished in a way that feels like a threat.
- No purchase pressure aimed at children.

## Random paid rewards

Random rewards bought with money (packs, crates, capsule draws) are central to many successful games. They create desire for things players did not know they wanted, multiply the things to chase, and feel good to open.

They are also the mechanic closest to gambling and the one drawing the most regulation. Rules in force somewhere include:

- Treating paid random rewards as gambling, which bans them.
- Banning compound draws, where a set of random pieces is needed to enter another draw.
- Requiring published odds.
- Raising the age rating of games that sell them, and restricting sales to minors.

These rules have tightened year by year and differ by country.

If the design uses them:

- Publish odds, per item or per rarity.
- Avoid compound structures that chain one random draw into another.
- Provide a direct or guaranteed route to any specific item (crafting from duplicates, a ceiling on unlucky streaks, direct purchase).
- Keep items that decide contests out of them.
- Consider not using them at all in games aimed at young players.
- Check the current rules in every market before launch. This reference is not legal advice.

## Review checklist

Ask these of any retention or monetization feature.

1. Would players approve of this mechanic if it were explained to them plainly?
2. Is every cost visible before the player commits?
3. Does the player know the odds?
4. Can the player stop without penalty at a natural point?
5. Does it reward coming back more than it punishes staying away?
6. Is the most affected player (youngest, most impulsive, most indebted) still treated fairly?
7. Will a buyer feel good about this purchase next week?
8. Is there a spending summary or limit a player can see or set?
9. Is anything here tuned per player in a way that exploits a weakness and not a preference?
10. Does it comply with the strictest rules among the markets we ship to?

Consider showing lifetime spend prominently and asking for explicit confirmation when someone goes on a spending run. One long-time player who added up his own spending on a favorite game found it was about double what he had guessed.

## How to respond to risky requests

When asked to design something that crosses a firm line (hidden odds, deceptive offers, difficulty that rises for payers, pressure aimed at children, withdrawal-style punishments):

1. Say plainly which line it crosses and what it would do to players and to the game's reputation.
2. Offer the nearest honest version that serves the same business goal. Examples: published odds with a guaranteed path, a generous starter purchase, a monthly calendar with no streak loss, optional rewarded ads, cosmetic prestige items.
3. Point out the practical risk as well: refunds, store policy, regulation, and players leaving.

For gray areas, lay out intent, method and outcome, give a recommendation, and let the user decide.
