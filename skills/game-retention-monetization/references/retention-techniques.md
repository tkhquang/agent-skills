# Retention techniques

Contents: principles, techniques, psychological effects in play, core loop types, superfan layer ingredients, choosing a set.

## Principles

- The retention layer is the strategy for long-term play. Return hooks are tactics. Build the first, then add the second.
- Prefer repeatable systems to expensive content. Art, narrative and ceremony amplify feelings the systems create.
- Ceremony matters: when the player achieves something, amplify the emotion with animation, sound and a pause in time. A famous casual game plays a triumphant classical chorus in slow motion as the last ball drops.
- Games give clearer evidence of progress than most of life does. These needs explain much of why people stay: competence (I am getting better), autonomy (my choices matter), relatedness (I am connected to others).

## Techniques

| Technique | How it holds players | Weakness or caution |
|---|---|---|
| Be really fun | The base layer is its own reward | Risky as the only plan. Novelty fades as platforms mature |
| High score | One more go to beat it | Needs a very strong base layer |
| Persistent high score | The personal best is always on screen | The better you get, the lower the odds of beating it. Eventually a wall |
| Leaderboards | Beat other people | One outlandish score demotivates everyone |
| Seasons | Boards reset weekly or monthly so anyone can climb again | Needs enough active players per bracket |
| Levels | Curiosity about the next one | A forward-looking design means making content forever |
| Getting to the end | Completion is a strong motive | An ending caps lifetime. A trade-off, not an error |
| Leveling up | Visible growth in power and options | Becomes a bare number treadmill without choices |
| Progress across a map | Progress shown in space, many future steps visible, friends shown along the path | A prettier map that hides the path loses the pull |
| Loops | Nested short, medium and long goals. Several timers and tasks always in progress | Too many open loops becomes anxiety |
| Real time | Things happen on the clock, even while away | Device clocks can be changed. Use a server clock when it matters |
| Appointment mechanics | The player chooses a task that completes at a time that suits their day, and feels committed to return | Punishing lateness (withered crops) has fallen out of favor |
| Narrative | "What happens next?" | Expensive per minute of play. Short, cheap, serialized formats can work |
| Star ratings | Replay old levels for a better grade | Appeals to a subset. Many players only look forward |
| Stars as currency | Grades buy upgrades, so replaying is useful to everyone and stuck players have a way to get stronger | Needs things worth buying |
| Challenges | Repeatable tasks that give goals and drip currency | Tune for return, not for longer sessions |
| Achievements | One-time feats that anchor goals and showcase other ways to play | Do not build the core around them |
| Social | Promises to other people: take your turn, show up for the group, repay a favor | Expensive to build. Sharing to a feed is not a social feature |

### Notes on particular techniques

Map design. A winding path that shows around fifty levels at once, with friends' portraits along it, does several jobs: visual reward, pull toward what is ahead, social proof, and a leaderboard hidden in plain sight.

Appointments versus virtual pets. In an appointment the player decides when to come back by choosing what to start. A virtual pet that demands attention on its own schedule removes that agency and breeds resentment. Fixed free rewards every few hours are weaker than appointments because nothing was promised.

Challenge cadence. Compare these designs.

- Completing an objective immediately produces another. This lengthens the session.
- One new quest arrives per day into three slots, each worth about half a pack of the game's main purchasable. A regular player earns a free pack every two days. Someone absent for three days starts missing out because the slots are full. This feels generous and brings people back through loss aversion.

Achievement types.

- Pointless: things that happen anyway (ten of something, a hundred of something). They mainly signal that the system exists.
- Reverse: play differently and reuse existing content. "Have five near misses." "Finish the night level without the plants made for night."
- Challenging: real feats that take planning and skill.

Narrative. Whether story drives players depends on the audience. As a main retention device it is costly for a game meant to be played daily for years. Seek repeatable systems first and let story amplify them.

Social. Sources of obligation: a friend waiting on your turn, a group activity scheduled for tonight, removal from the alliance if inactive, a favor that should be returned. Overuse (begging for gifts in every session) eventually reads as manipulation.

## Psychological effects in play

| Effect | Meaning | Use | Care |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unfinished tasks stick | Started and uncompleted tasks stay in mind | Open loops, progress bars | Leave an honest stopping point |
| Endowed progress | A head start makes people more likely to finish. A ten-stamp card with two pre-stamped is completed far more often than a blank eight-stamp card | Start meters partly filled | Do not fake progress the player did not earn in a way that feels deceptive |
| Loss aversion | Losing hurts about twice as much as gaining pleases | Streaks, full quest slots | A painful loss can end the relationship |
| Commitment and consistency | People keep promises they made | Appointments, guild roles | The promise must be the player's own |
| Reciprocity | Favors create a wish to repay | Gifts, help requests | Spam destroys it |
| Social proof | People do what others do | Friends on the map, visible groups | Must be real |
| Scarcity | Limited things are wanted more | Limited events and items | Artificial urgency erodes trust |
| Variable rewards | Unpredictable payoffs hold attention strongly | Loot, drops | The highest-risk tool. See the ethics reference |

## Core loop types

| Type | Shape | Typical games | Design focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linear | Unique levels in order, story advancing, no choices in the long-term layer | Narrative and mission games | Drawing the player through. Costly to produce |
| Strategic | Choices in both layers and in what moves between them, with effects running both ways | Strategy and tactical games with a campaign | What you bring into a fight, what you bring back, and how failure changes the larger picture |
| Replayable | The same or procedurally varied round played many times to feed progression | Endless runners, card battlers, match-based games | Variety in each round and a strong reason for the next |

A middling base layer can be lifted by an excellent retention layer. A matching-puzzle game became a chart regular by letting each completed level restore part of a neglected garden.

Thinking of the long-term layer as the ordinary world and the round as an adventure helps with the transitions: make crossing into play and returning from it clear and symmetrical.

## Superfan layer ingredients

Games with the highest spend per player tend to share a social, competitive endgame.

- Groups: guilds, leagues, alliances. They add purpose, mutual obligation and a reason to spend "for the team".
- Competition between groups, directly or through boards.
- Player against player, live or asynchronous.
- Visible status and character growth.

Lighter versions exist: completing a full collection, earning every achievement. Devoted players also invent their own layer (hiring other players, politics, espionage). You enable it more than you control it.

## Choosing a set

1. Pick one or two primary structures that suit the base layer (a map of levels, or collection and upgrade, or seasonal competition).
2. Add nested goals at short, medium and long timescales.
3. Add at most one time-based mechanic that respects agency.
4. Decide what, if anything, serves the most devoted players.
5. Leave challenges, achievements, login rewards, events and notifications until the loop has shown it retains without them.
