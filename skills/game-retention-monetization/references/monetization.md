# Monetization

Contents: who pays, what is sold, why people buy, virtual goods checklist, pay-to-win, seasonal pass worked design, advertising, free players, store review.

## Who pays

Spend is extremely concentrated.

- In one month across many mobile games, about 1.5 percent of players paid anything. The top tenth of those payers produced about half of revenue.
- On one web game platform, about 2 percent of players ever paid. The 4 percent of payers who spent over 500 dollars produced 72 percent of revenue. Mean lifetime spend was 117 dollars and the median was 10.

Consequences:

- Averages mislead. Spend follows a power law. Compare mean, median and mode: the further apart, the more concentrated.
- The design must allow high spend. At a 1 percent conversion rate, payers need to average about a hundred times what "everyone pays a little" would have required.
- Repeat purchases are the business. The heaviest spenders buy more often as well as bigger.

The formula: let the players who love your game spend lots of money on things they really value. Every part matters. People who do not love it will not pay. A low ceiling wastes the willingness of those who do. Things they do not value cause regret and end the relationship.

Say superfans, not whales, and self-expression, not vanity items. The words shape what gets designed.

## What is sold

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Soft currency | Earned in play, gates ordinary progress | Coins, gold |
| Hard currency | Scarce, mostly bought, skips time or buys premium things | Gems |
| Consumables | Used once | Boosts, extra moves, refills |
| Durables | Permanent benefit | Earnings doubler, extra builder, more queue slots, larger life pool |
| Cosmetics | Appearance | Outfits, skins, animations |
| Random packs | Chance at items | Card packs, crates. See the ethics reference |
| Content | More game | Expansions, episodes, characters |
| Time | Finish now instead of later | Skip a timer |
| Seasonal pass | A priced track of rewards earned by playing during a season | Battle pass, season pass |

## Why people buy

- Self-expression. Showing who they are. Needs an audience.
- Status and conspicuous display. The same card with a rarer finish at four times the cost. Humans signal by spending on the unnecessary.
- Power and progress. Getting stronger or getting there faster.
- Relationships. Gifts, and spending so as not to let the group down. A purchase "for the team" feels generous where the same purchase for oneself might feel selfish. An item that showers rewards only other players can pick up turns its buyer into the life of the party.
- Belonging and celebration. A one-dollar seasonal hat sold a million copies in a week in a racing game where everyone could see it. It satisfied being first, standing out, fitting in and sharing a moment.
- Completion. Finishing a collection.
- Content. More of what they love.

## Virtual goods checklist

These attributes decide whether a virtual item sells.

- Display: can other players see it? A studio added avatar customization and sold nothing, because nobody could see anyone else's avatar. A shooter solved the "hidden player" problem by showing the victor to the player they just defeated.
- Agency: does it do something, or is it worn by something that acts?
- Permanence: does it leave a lasting mark? Consumables sell best when they contribute to something permanent, such as a rank or trophy.

A trading interface that sends buyers to the seller's home instead of an efficient list is slower and better: every sale becomes a visit, a chance to be seen and a reason to decorate.

## Pay-to-win

- If spending guarantees victory, non-spenders leave and the spenders have nobody to beat.
- If purchases do nothing, they are hard to sell.
- The workable middle: sell advantages in the long-term layer (faster experience, more currency per round, more options) and not in the moment-to-moment contest (damage, health). Players regard the round as where skill lives. Buying time is accepted. Buying the result is not.
- Owning everything should still lose to playing well.
- In competitive games part of the value of owning more is knowledge: you learn a character's weaknesses by playing it.
- Some players call any purchase pay-to-win. They are not the audience for this model.

## Seasonal pass, worked design

Every number here is an illustration to replace with your own.

A pass bundles things the game may already have: a season (a reset that lets anyone climb again), a visible track of tiers, challenges that feed the track, and a price. It amplifies a loop that already retains. Add it after the core has held players without it.

Structure:

- A free track and a paid track over the same tiers. The free track must be worth having, because free players are the audience and the opposition.
- Sell expression and speed on the paid track, never victory.
- Let players buy at any point and receive every paid reward for tiers already reached. The buyer then knows exactly what the money gets.
- Optionally return enough premium currency along the track to pay for the next pass. This gives buyers a reason to finish every season.

Pace it backward from a target player:

1. Season length and size: 10 weeks, 50 tiers, 1,000 points each, so 50,000 points.
2. Who should finish, and when: someone playing four days a week finishes in week 8, with two weeks of slack.
3. Sources: weekly challenges worth 4,000 points that stay available all season once released, and ordinary play worth up to 600 points a day, capped.
4. Check each kind of player:

| Player | Points per week | Finishes |
|---|---|---|
| Four days a week | 4,000 + 4 x 600 = 6,400 | Week 8 |
| Every day | 4,000 + 7 x 600 = 8,200 | Week 7 |
| Two days a week, all challenges done | 4,000 + 2 x 600 = 5,200 | Week 10, only just |
| Joins in week 6, four days a week | 40,000 from all ten weeks of challenges + 20 x 600 | Before the end |

Reading the table:

- The daily cap keeps the most active player one week ahead, so nobody is pushed to play all day.
- Challenges that accumulate let a late or lapsed player catch up. Daily tasks that expire would lock them out.
- If the two-days-a-week player matters, this pass is too tight.

Keeping it honest:

- Points come mostly from playing the way the player already wanted to. Challenges that force a disliked mode turn the pass into a chore list.
- Show tiers reached, days left and where the current pace ends. A player who cannot finish should learn that in week 3.
- Expiry works through loss aversion. Use it lightly. Passes that never expire, or rewards that return later, give up some urgency and most of the resentment.
- Selling tier skips is acceptable when finishing by play is realistic for the target player. A pass tuned so buyers must also buy skips makes the game harder for people who paid.
- Any tier that gives a random reward needs stated odds.

Measures: share of active players who buy, share of buyers who finish, the spread of final tiers, retention at the season boundary, and repurchase next season. Few buyers finishing means the purchase felt bad. A drop in play right after the season ends means the pass was carrying retention the loop should carry.

## Advertising

- Rewarded, optional video is welcomed where interruption is resented. Offering to double a run's rewards for watching an ad makes players ask for the ad.
- Limiting ads per session turns them into a resource and a natural stopping point.
- "Pay to remove ads" becomes a disincentive if ads are rewards.
- Most ads are for other games, so you may be selling your best players to competitors. Weigh that.
- Ads make a large audience with low willingness to spend viable.

## Free players

People who never pay are valuable.

- They are opponents and teammates. In multiplayer they are part of the content.
- They may convert later.
- They tell friends and lift chart position.
- They can earn ad revenue.
- They are the audience that gives display purchases their meaning.

## Store review

- Is there an obvious, generous first purchase that a mildly interested player would be glad they made?
- Is there something meaningful to buy at 5, 20 and 100 times that price?
- Can a devoted player keep finding things they value month after month?
- Does every item pass display, agency and permanence, or have a deliberate reason not to?
- Does anything sold decide the outcome of a contest between players?
- Would a buyer feel good about each purchase a week later?
- Are odds stated wherever chance is involved?
- Is spending for other people possible?
