# Concept document

Contents: purpose and rules, section guide, fill-in template, quality checks.

## Purpose and rules

The concept document states the vision clearly enough that a team, a funder or a stranger gets the same picture. It is informative and persuasive at once.

- Keep it short: one to three pages, linking out to detailed design documents.
- Keep it alive. A stale concept document is worse than none. Update it when the design changes.
- Use pictures and diagrams where they say more than text.
- Write it after you have at least one playable test of the core, or mark every claim that is still a guess.

## Section guide

### High-level concept

Working title. A convenient handle. It will probably change, so do not spend long on it.

Concept statement. One or two sentences, about the length of a short social post, that tell someone who knows nothing what the player does, why it is different and why it is fun. Expect several drafts.

The One Question. A single question that resolves feature debates for this game. "Does it rock?" for a rhythm game about being a rock star. "Is it an authentic experience of being in a band?" for its sequel. Write it once and use it in every review.

Genre. Say which aspects of each genre you mean, in terms of what the player's attention is on: "The multiple-unit control of a strategy game and the pace of an action game, with the easy-to-learn interactions of a casual game."

- Fitting one established genre too neatly makes a forgettable game.
- A mashup that takes a paragraph to explain will not be understood.

Target audience. Describe it by:

- Motivations: which of action, social, mastery, achievement, immersion, creativity this game serves. They cluster as action plus social, mastery plus achievement, immersion plus creativity. Avoid combining motivations that rarely coexist, such as a delicate emotional story and constant destruction.
- Demographics, only where they matter. Useful trends: appetite for fast excitement falls steadily with age; appetite for competition falls fastest and is low by about forty; the urge to complete things stays strong at every age.
- Context: platform, typical session length, where and when they play, how experienced they are.

"Everyone" is not an audience. Name the bull's-eye.

Unique selling points. Three to five short statements. Test each with one question: why would someone stop playing a game they already like to play this one? Surface changes do not count. Do not stitch together selling points from other games; they must serve one vision. Players do not care how hard you worked.

### Product description

Player experience.

- The fantasy: who the player is and what situation they are in.
- Key moments: three to five short anecdotes told from the player's seat, covering the first five minutes, the first win, the first loss, a moment of mastery. Write them for different kinds of player.
- Emotions and meaning: what the player feels during and after, and what the game says, if anything.

Visual and audio style. A few references and adjectives. Enough to set tone.

World fiction. The premise in a paragraph.

Monetization. The business model and what, if anything, is sold.

Technology and platform. Engine, target devices, anything risky.

Scope. Team size, time, content count. State what is deliberately out.

Intentional choices. List every choice that a later reader could mistake for a defect, such as no timer, unlimited undo or a small board. For each: the choice, why it exists, what the opposite breaks. A reviewer or an agent may not reverse one without the owner.

### Detailed design pointers

One paragraph each, linking to fuller documents when they exist.

- Core loops: what the player does most of the time and what brings them back.
- Objectives and progression: short, medium and long-term goals.
- Main systems: combat, economy, crafting, social and so on.
- Interactivity: which kinds of engagement the game leans on (fast action, puzzle solving, long-term planning, social, emotional).

## Quality checks before sharing

- A stranger can repeat the concept statement after one read.
- The entrance test passes: given only the statement, someone describes roughly the game you mean.
- Each selling point survives "why would I leave my current game for this?".
- The key moments are about what the player does and feels, not about features.
- The scope section names something that was cut.
- Every unproven claim has a test attached.

## Shorter forms

- Elevator pitch: the concept statement plus the strongest selling point, spoken in under thirty seconds.
- One-pager: concept statement, audience, three selling points, one key moment, scope.
- Proposal (five to twenty pages): the concept document plus budget, schedule, competitive analysis and plans beyond launch.
