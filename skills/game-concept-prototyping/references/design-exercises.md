# Design exercises

Contents: how to run one, exercise format, exercise bank, timed group format.

Short, constrained, no-code exercises for practicing design or shaking loose a stuck project. Each one produces something playable on a table in under two hours. Use them as warm-ups, as interview-style tests, or as a way to explore one design question in isolation.

## How to run one with a user

1. Pick an exercise that isolates the skill they want (or the problem their game has).
2. State the brief, the constraints and the deliverable. Constraints are the point: do not relax them.
3. Follow the suggested process. Insist on a playtest, even a solo one, after every added mechanic. Ask each time: Is it more or less fun? Does it support the core? Does it work the way I expected?
4. Finish with a short written analysis: what changed in the dynamics, and whether the change made the game better, worse or just different.
5. Offer a variant that raises the difficulty.

Habits to enforce: do not write the rules down until you have to (if the designer cannot hold them in their head, players will not either), and when a fix needs a fix, remove the original mechanic instead of stacking patches.

## Exercise format

```markdown
## [Title]
Brief: [The situation and the goal, two to four sentences.]
Constraints: [Hard limits. Player count, components, time, rule length.]
Components: [What is needed on the table.]
Deliverable: [Playable prototype / written rules / one-page design / analysis.]
Process:
1. [First step]
2. [Playtest]
3. [Revise]
Variants: [Harder or different versions.]
```

## Exercise bank

### Foundations

- The path. Two to four players race from A to B. Design the theme, the pieces and the rules. Requirement: players must be able to help or hinder each other, and every such move must cost something. Variant: design it again starting from mechanics instead of theme and compare the two processes.
- It's mine. A territory game for two to four players. Win by holding the most after a fixed number of turns.
- Explore. A game about revealing an unknown space, physical or otherwise.
- One more atom. Take a trivial card game (draw one, play one, first to complete a set wins). Add exactly one of: a production cost, sabotage, a random event deck. Compare.

### Chance

- Luck in a solved game. Add a chance element to a fully solved children's game so that adults want to play it. Rolling a die to pick your move does not count.
- The randomly driven extra player. Take a game that plays badly with three because two gang up on one. Add a fourth "player" driven only by a shuffled deck of action cards.
- Rules on a business card. Design a two-player card game whose full rules fit on one small card and can be taught in two minutes.

### Decisions and strategy

- Skill from nowhere. Take a pure-luck dice game. Add one decision that makes it interesting for adults while children can still play. Candidates: an auction, a push-your-luck choice, a bet on the outcome.
- Game system. Using only one fixed set of generic components (tiles, tokens, pawns, dice), design a 60 to 90 minute game with simple rules, little downtime and meaningful choices.
- Decision audit. Take any game. List every decision a player makes in ten minutes and label each: obvious, meaningless, blind, tradeoff, dilemma, risk versus reward. Redesign one bad decision into a tradeoff.

### Dexterity and time pressure

- Add twitch to strategy. Add a physical or timed element to a pure strategy game and describe how the dynamics changed. A turn timer is the obvious answer: find a less obvious one.
- Speed dice. A five-minute game using only dice, with speed and luck and no strategy.
- Under pressure. Make a tabletop version of a video game built on avoidance, and make it harder over time using a real timer.

### Tuning the luck and skill mix

- Simplify for children. Take a strategy board game and rework it for ages five to eight: replace decisions with chance, cut rules to a few paragraphs, finish in thirty minutes.
- Grow up a children's game. Take a pure-luck children's game and add just enough choice for adults to enjoy it with children. Do not go so far that the children lose interest.

### Puzzles

- Defuse it. Design a timed puzzle that fits a "defuse the device" theme. Propose three different puzzle types that suit the theme, then prototype one. An arbitrary sliding-tile puzzle attached to the theme fails the brief.
- Clue ladder. Take a puzzle with one hidden solution. Write three successive hints and one alternate solution, then test whether fresh solvers still feel clever.

### Adding and removing

- One new component. Add a new component (a second kind of die, a second deck) to a classic card game so it is still recognizable and feels like it belongs to a different world.
- Kill a mechanic. Remove one core mechanic from a familiar genre and design what replaces the lost tension.
- Core check. For an existing design, list every feature and mark whether it strengthens the core. Cut two that do not and describe what improves.

### Interface

- Many avatars, one controller. Design the screens and controls for a team sport video game. List every play state, the actions available in each and the information needed in each, then lay out one screen per state.
- Paper interface. Build a game screen from index cards on a board. Have someone use a finger as the cursor. Move cards until they stop hesitating.

### Story

- Interview a character. Invent a character with a time, place, past and plans. Answer an interview as them: origins, struggles, how a stranger would stereotype them, strengths, weaknesses, two defining moments.
- Do, don't show. Take a scene normally told as a cutscene and redesign it so the player causes or takes part in it.
- Work backward. Start from the final goal and chain questions back to the start: "Why can't I do that yet? Because X. Where is X? With Y."
- One story, three treatments. Take a well-known story and outline it as three different genres. For each, name the core dynamic and three mechanics that deliver it.

### Casual

- Not another match-three. Innovate on a matching game without reskinning it. Prototype with cards.
- Known pattern, overlapped. Find an everyday sequence of steps everyone already knows and build a click-management game from running several copies at once.

## Timed group format

For a team warm-up or an interview: one brief, a hard time limit of 30 to 60 minutes, small groups, then each group presents the pitch, the rules and one thing they cut. The time limit is the constraint that matters most.
